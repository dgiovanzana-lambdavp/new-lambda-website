# How Much Growth Debt Can a GovTech Software Company Take On?

**The right amount of debt is not the most a lender will give you. It is the amount your slowest paying agency cannot break.**

*By Domenic Giovanzana, Founder and General Partner*

I get this question right after founders decide they would rather borrow than raise. Lenders that finance software companies usually size growth debt off recurring revenue, not profits, and the amount moves with the quality of that revenue: retention, customer concentration, contract length, and gross margin. Any specific multiple you hear is one lender's offer, not a market rule. For a GovTech company, large agency customers and slow government payments can shrink the number and make cash covenants the real constraint.

This is the second post in a series on growth capital. The first compared [venture debt and venture capital for GovTech](https://www.lambdavp.com/blog/venture-debt-vs-venture-capital-govtech).

*This article is general information, not legal, tax, or investment advice.*

## How lenders size growth debt: multiples of recurring revenue, not EBITDA

Start with the line most founders skip: your recurring revenue, not your profit, sets the size of the loan. Underridge describes the mechanics plainly: "Recurring revenue loans are typically sized as a multiple of ARR or recurring revenue, with the multiple influenced by gross retention, growth, gross margin, customer concentration and the path to positive cash flow." ([Underridge](https://www.underridge.com/library/deferred-revenue-and-saas-metrics))

Why revenue and not earnings? Growing software companies spend ahead of revenue, so EBITDA is often low or negative even when the customer base is valuable. That is exactly the gap these loans were built to fill, and the better your growth and retention, the larger the loan you can expect.

The multiple itself is negotiated deal by deal. I would treat any number quoted before a lender has seen your customer data as a starting point, not a promise.

## Why gross margin and services revenue matter

Not all revenue counts the same in a lender's eyes, and services revenue counts least. Maxio's guide on evaluating debt puts it simply: "The lower your gross margin, the lower your ARR debt ceiling should be." ([Maxio](https://www.maxio.com/blog/evaluating-debt-options-for-saas-companies))

That matters for GovTech. Implementation work and hosting pass through costs pull margin down, and one time implementation fees are not recurring revenue at all. If your services revenue is large, see how to [get paid during implementation](https://www.lambdavp.com/blog/government-software-implementation-payment-milestones) before you size a loan against it.

## What lenders underwrite: retention, concentration, contract term

Your strongest asset in a lender meeting is how rarely your customers leave, and you have to prove it on paper. Inflection CFO describes what lenders actually do with that: "Lenders build renewal risk models. They ask for detailed customer contract schedules showing maturity dates, termination rights, and expansion potential." ([Inflection CFO](https://inflectioncfo.co/blog/venture-debt-revenue-concentration-the-customer-risk-trap-lenders-wont-tell-you/))

Gross retention is the number I would lead with, because it shows how much revenue survives if new sales stop. Concentration is the number to get ahead of. A single large state agency can make your revenue look concentrated even when the relationship is decades old, so bring the renewal history that proves it is stable.

Bring the positives with you: multiyear contracts, your renewal history, and your gross retention by year.

## Government contract clauses lenders will ask about

Government contracts come with exit clauses that commercial contracts do not, and a lender will want to know which of yours carry them. JurisState explains that a non appropriation exit often limits what a contractor can recover, while a termination for convenience generally pays for work performed plus a reasonable profit, and that lenders view non appropriation clauses as political risk that can raise interest rates ([JurisState](https://jurisstate.blog/non-appropriation-clause-government-contracts)).

My advice: map every contract by clause, funding source, and renewal date before the first lender call. It turns a vague worry into a schedule a lender can underwrite.

## Covenants: the strings attached to growth debt

The covenant I worry about most for GovTech founders is the minimum cash floor, because government payment timing is outside your control. Underridge describes the usual package: "Covenants commonly include minimum liquidity, minimum recurring revenue or retention, and a scheduled conversion to leverage and coverage covenants once the company reaches profitability." ([Underridge](https://www.underridge.com/library/deferred-revenue-and-saas-metrics))

The cash floor bites hardest early, while the business is still investing ahead of revenue. With receivables that can run for months, one slow agency can push your balance toward the floor while the business is fundamentally healthy. Three things I would negotiate:

1. **A trailing measurement.** Test the covenant on an average balance over a rolling quarter, not on a single day.
2. **A cure period,** so one late agency payment is not an immediate default.
3. **Carve outs and step downs.** Ask for exceptions for one time events and for covenants that loosen as you hit milestones.

Read the rest of the agreement too: cash sweeps and balloon payments can matter as much as the headline covenant.

Shortening the payment wait helps too: see [prompt payment laws for GovTech vendors](https://www.lambdavp.com/blog/prompt-payment-laws-govtech-software-vendors) and [government contract financing for software companies](https://www.lambdavp.com/blog/government-contract-financing-for-software-companies).

## Term loan vs revenue based financing vs venture debt: which sizing model fits

I would pick the structure that matches how your cash actually arrives. FinHelp puts the tradeoff simply: "Term loans provide predictable monthly obligations. RBF payments vary with revenue, easing strain in slow months." ([FinHelp](https://finhelp.io/glossary/term-loans-vs-revenue-based-financing-which-fits-your-business/))

Revenue based financing usually closes faster with fewer covenants, and you pay more for that flexibility. Classic venture debt is built for companies with VC backing, so for a bootstrapped GovTech company it is usually the wrong comparison.

## A simple way to estimate your own number

This is the worksheet I would run before talking to any lender. It uses no outside figures; plug in the terms you are actually quoted.

1. **Start with recurring revenue only.** Leave out implementation and other one time fees.
2. **Discount concentration.** Mark down any customer large enough that a lender would treat it as concentrated.
3. **Apply the multiple you are quoted** to what is left.
4. **Model debt service and every covenant** against a downside case and your slowest government payment cycle.
5. **Borrow only what you can justify** with a clear return on the money.

Upfront annual billing improves your cash position and covenant headroom; see whether [government customers can pay your SaaS subscription upfront](https://www.lambdavp.com/blog/government-saas-upfront-payment).

## Frequently asked questions

### How much can a SaaS company borrow?

There is no single answer. Be wary of any firm number quoted before someone has seen your financials. Lenders generally size recurring revenue loans as a multiple of ARR, and the multiple moves with gross retention, growth, gross margin, customer concentration, and how close you are to positive cash flow.

### Why do lenders lend against ARR instead of profits?

Growing software companies often spend ahead of revenue, so EBITDA can be low or negative even when the customer base is valuable. Les Barclays, citing White & Case, describes ARR financing as built for borrowers with little or no EBITDA to support a conventional loan.

### What do lenders look at when underwriting SaaS revenue?

Gross retention, churn, customer concentration, contract length, and gross margin. Gross retention tends to carry the most weight because it shows how much revenue would remain if new sales stopped. Expect to hand over a contract schedule with renewal dates and termination rights.

### Does having one large government customer reduce how much I can borrow?

It can. Lenders discount revenue that depends on a few large accounts, and a big state agency can look like concentration risk even after years of renewals. Government contracts also add non appropriation risk, which JurisState says lenders view as political risk that can raise interest rates.

### What covenants come with growth debt?

Usually a minimum liquidity or cash floor, a minimum recurring revenue or retention test, and sometimes a later switch to leverage and coverage tests once the company is profitable. Term loans can also carry cash sweeps or balloon payments, so read every covenant before you sign.

### Why is a minimum cash covenant risky for a GovTech company?

Government payment timing is outside your control, so one slow agency can push cash toward the floor while the business is healthy. Inflection CFO suggests measuring the covenant on an average over a rolling quarter rather than a single day, and asking for a cure period.

### Is revenue based financing or a term loan better for a software company?

FinHelp notes that term loans give predictable monthly payments while revenue based payments vary with revenue, easing strain in slow months. Revenue based financing usually costs more for that flexibility. Uneven government receipts can make the flexibility worth paying for.

### How much debt is too much?

Too much is any amount you cannot service if new sales stall and an agency pays late. Maxio warns that the lower your gross margin, the lower your debt ceiling should be. Model debt service and every covenant against a downside plan and your slowest government payment cycle.
