# When the Government Pays Late: Prompt Payment Laws Every GovTech Software Vendor Should Know

**The law says the government owes you interest when it pays late. It does not say the government will make your payroll.**

*By Domenic Giovanzana, Founder and General Partner. Last updated October 11, 2026.*

Federal agencies generally must pay a proper invoice within 30 days and owe interest automatically if they pay late. State and local rules differ: some set 30, 45 or 60 day deadlines with statutory interest, and disputes or improper invoices usually stop the clock. Interest gives a vendor a small amount of compensation. It does not replace the cash while you wait.

I talk with a lot of GovTech founders who run real businesses: a few million in revenue, growing steadily, government customers who renew. Many of them carry receivables of 90 to 180 days and assume there is a legal remedy that fixes it. There is a remedy. It is just smaller and narrower than most people expect. This post walks through what the federal and state prompt payment rules actually say, what they recover, and what they leave on your plate.

## The federal rule in plain English

Under the federal Prompt Payment Act, as implemented in the FAR payment clause, the due date is generally "the later of" two events: the 30th day after the billing office receives a proper invoice, or the 30th day after the government accepts the goods or services. ([FAR 52.232-25](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/52.232-25)) If your contract names a specific payment date, that date governs. ([Bureau of the Fiscal Service FAQ](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/payments-from-government/prompt-payment/faqs))

Three things to know:

- **Interest is automatic.** The FAR clause says the payment office "will pay an interest penalty automatically, without request from the Contractor, if payment is not made by the due date" and the clause's conditions are met. ([FAR 52.232-25](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/52.232-25))
- **Treasury sets the rate.** The Bureau of the Fiscal Service publishes the rate by period. For July 1 through December 31, 2026, it is 4.75%. ([Bureau of the Fiscal Service](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/fsservices/gov/pmt/promptPayment/promptPayment_home.htm)) Check the rate in effect when your invoice became late.
- **Interest runs from the day after the due date.** Treasury guidance says the penalty is paid "starting on the day after the required payment date and ending on the date on which payment is made." ([Treasury Financial Manual, Chapter 5000](https://tfx.treasury.gov/tfm/volume1/part4a/chapter-5000-prompt-payment-act-ppa-guidance-heads-government-departments))

## The "proper invoice" trap

For software vendors, this is the section that matters most. The 30 day clock does not start until the agency has a proper invoice.

Treasury puts it plainly: "The payment period starts when the agency receives a proper invoice that includes all required information." If an invoice is improper, the agency must return it "no later than 7 days after receipt," list the defects, and ask for a corrected invoice. No late payment interest is due until the payment period runs out after the corrected invoice arrives. ([Bureau of the Fiscal Service FAQ](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/payments-from-government/prompt-payment/faqs))

Small details can make an invoice improper. Treasury notes that if a payment is rejected "because the EFT information is not correct, it is an improper invoice." ([Bureau of the Fiscal Service FAQ](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/payments-from-government/prompt-payment/faqs))

Invoice hygiene that starts the clock:

- Match every invoice to what your contract requires (contract and order numbers, line items, billing office address).
- Bill against the acceptance event your contract defines, such as go-live or a milestone sign-off, and keep the acceptance record.
- Keep your banking and taxpayer information current with the agency.
- Record the date each invoice was received. If an invoice comes back, resubmit fast and mark it as corrected.

Your contract, not a blog post, defines what "proper" means for your agency. Read the invoicing clause before you send the first bill.

## When interest does not apply: disputes and withholdings

The automatic interest has a large exception. Under the FAR, the government does not have to pay interest penalties "if payment delays are due to disagreement between the Government and the Contractor over the payment amount or other issues involving contract compliance, or on amounts temporarily withheld or retained in accordance with the terms of the contract." Those claims are resolved under the Disputes clause. ([FAR 52.232-25](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/52.232-25))

If you believe you are owed an additional penalty because the interest itself was not paid properly, the clause requires a written demand "postmarked not later than 40 days after the invoice amount is paid," with supporting documents including a copy of the invoice. ([FAR 52.232-25](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/52.232-25)) Miss that window and you lose the claim.

## Faster than 30 days: accelerated payment for small businesses

Treasury says agencies should pay early when they have a proper invoice, it is in the government's best interest, and one of three conditions is true: the invoice is under $2,500, the payment is to a small business, or the payment relates to an emergency, disaster, or military deployment. ([Bureau of the Fiscal Service](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/fsservices/gov/pmt/promptPayment/promptPayment_home.htm))

"Should" is not "must." If you qualify as a small business, it is fair to ask your contracting officer whether accelerated payment applies to your invoices.

## State and local rules vary: five states compared

Most GovTech companies sell to states, counties, cities and school districts, not only federal agencies. There is no single rule. Here is what the statutes say in a few states, as of October 2026.

| Minnesota municipalities | Per contract, else within 35 days of receipt (45 days if the governing board meets less than monthly) | 1.5% per month or part of a month | No interest during a good faith dispute | [Minn. Stat. 471.425](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/2025/cite/471.425) |
| Virginia localities | Per contract, else not more than 45 days after receipt of goods or services or the invoice, whichever is later | Finance charges assessed by the supplier, not to exceed 1% per month, unless the contract says otherwise | Locality must flag invoice defects within 20 days | [Va. | Texas governmental entities | Overdue on the 31st day after the later of delivery, completed service, or invoice receipt (46th day for some political subdivisions) | Prime rate plus 1%, set each September 1 | Vendor may suspend work for unpaid undisputed amounts after written notice, no earlier than the 10th day | [Tex. Gov't Code 2251.021](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._gov't_code_section_2251.021), [2251.025](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._gov't_code_section_2251.025), [2251.051](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._gov't_code_section_2251.051) |
| Florida local governments (non-construction) | 45 days after the date set in s. 218.73 | 1% per month on the unpaid balance, starting 30 days after the due date | Vendor must invoice for the interest | [Fla. Stat. 218.74](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/218.74) |

A few details are worth calling out.

**Minnesota** is one of the stronger statutes for vendors. State agencies pay 1.5% per month on undisputed late bills, but "before any interest payment is made, the vendor must invoice the state agency for such interest." ([Minn. Stat. 16A.124](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/16A.124)) If you never ask, you never get paid.

**Virginia** frames the local remedy as finance charges "assessed by the supplier." ([Va. Code 2.2-4352](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title2.2/chapter43/section2.2-4352/)) In practice that means you assess them; the locality is not obligated to volunteer them.

**Texas** gives vendors a lever most states do not: the right to suspend performance on an undisputed unpaid amount after written notice, and not before "the 10th day after the date the vendor gives notice." ([Tex. Gov't Code 2251.051](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._gov't_code_section_2251.051)) Suspending a live government software deployment is a serious step. Talk to counsel first.

**Florida** counties and cities owe interest on late non-construction payments, but it starts "from 30 days after the due date," and "the vendor must invoice the local governmental entity for any interest accrued." ([Fla. Stat. 218.74](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/218.74))

Always check your own state's statute and your contract. Coverage differs between state agencies, localities and school districts, and contracts can change the defaults.

## What interest actually recovers

Here is the rough math. Statutory interest is approximately:

**unpaid principal × annual rate × (days late / 365)**

That is a simplified version; federal interest, for example, is calculated with Treasury's own calculator and conventions. As an illustration only, using made-up numbers: a $100,000 federal invoice paid 60 days late at 4.75% earns roughly $780 in interest.

Now compare that with what the 60 days cost you. If your team, hosting and support cost tens of thousands of dollars a month, the interest is a rounding error against the cash you carried. And it arrives at the end, with the late payment, not while you are making payroll.

State rates of 1% or 1.5% per month are higher than the federal rate, but the same two limits apply: you only get it after you are paid, and disputes usually pause it.

## A practical playbook when an agency or city pays late

1. **Confirm the invoice was proper and received.** Get the receipt date in writing.
2. **Ask in writing for status and any defects.** A polite email to the billing office and your contracting officer creates a record.
3. **Document delivery, acceptance and any dispute notices.** You will need these for any interest claim.
4. **Invoice for interest where the statute requires it.** Minnesota state agencies and Florida local governments, for example, require the vendor to invoice for the interest; Virginia localities pay finance charges you assess.
5. **Know your suspension rights, if any.** Texas has one; many states do not. Get legal advice before using it.
6. **On federal contracts, escalate through the contracting officer and the Disputes clause,** and track the 40 day window for additional penalty demands.

## What the law does not fix: the cash gap

Prompt payment laws make late payment slightly more expensive for the government. They do not shorten the time between signature and payment, they do not cover the implementation months before you can invoice, and they stop working when there is a dispute.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does the federal government have to pay a vendor under the Prompt Payment Act?

Generally the later of 30 days after the billing office receives a proper invoice or 30 days after the government accepts the goods or services. If the contract specifies a payment date, that date governs. The clock only starts once the agency has a proper invoice with all required information.

### Do I have to ask the federal government for late payment interest?

No. Under FAR 52.232-25 the payment office pays the interest penalty automatically if payment is late and the clause's conditions are met. If you believe an additional penalty is owed, you must make a written demand postmarked no later than 40 days after the invoice amount is paid.

### What is the Prompt Payment Act interest rate in 2026?

The Bureau of the Fiscal Service lists 4.75% for July 1 through December 31, 2026. Treasury publishes the rate by period, so check the rate in effect when your invoice became late, and use Treasury's prompt payment calculator for the exact figure.

### What makes an invoice "improper" under the federal rules?

An invoice missing information the agency requires is improper. The agency must return it no later than 7 days after receipt, explain the defects and request a corrected invoice. Interest only becomes due after the payment period runs on the corrected invoice. Incorrect EFT information can also make an invoice improper.

### Does a dispute stop late payment interest?

Usually, yes. Federal rules do not require interest when the delay comes from disagreement over the payment amount or contract compliance, or from amounts withheld under the contract. Minnesota also limits interest during disputes, and other states have their own rules. Check your statute and contract for how disputes are handled.

### Do state and local governments have prompt payment laws for software vendors?

Many do, and they vary. Minnesota, Virginia, New Jersey, Texas and Florida all have statutes covering vendor payments, with different deadlines, interest rates and claim steps. Some apply to state agencies and some to localities. Always read your state's statute and your contract's payment terms.

### Can I stop work if a government customer does not pay?

It depends on state law and your contract. Texas, for example, lets a vendor suspend performance when an undisputed amount goes unpaid, after written notice and no earlier than the 10th day after that notice. Many states have no such right. Talk to counsel before suspending any service.

### Is late payment interest enough to cover the cost of waiting for government payment?

In my view, rarely. Interest accrues only after the due date, is paid with the late payment rather than during the wait, and usually stops during disputes. It does not fund payroll or hosting while you wait, which is why many vendors pair it with early payment programs or contract-backed financing.
