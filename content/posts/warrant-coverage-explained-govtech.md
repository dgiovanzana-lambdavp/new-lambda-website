# Warrant Coverage Explained for GovTech CEOs

**A warrant costs you nothing on signing day. That is exactly why founders underprice it.**

*By Domenic Giovanzana, Founder and General Partner*

Warrant coverage is the size of the equity option a lender takes alongside a loan, expressed as a percentage of the loan amount, not of your company. The warrant gives the lender the right, but not the obligation, to buy shares at a fixed strike price before it expires. Lenders typically exercise around a sale or IPO, and only if the shares are worth more than the strike. For a bootstrapped GovTech company that has not priced a round in years, how the strike is set can matter as much as the coverage percentage itself.

This is the third post in our growth capital series, after [venture debt vs venture capital for GovTech](https://www.lambdavp.com/blog/venture-debt-vs-venture-capital-govtech) and [how much growth debt a GovTech software company can take on](https://www.lambdavp.com/blog/how-much-growth-debt-govtech-software-company).

## What warrant coverage is, in one example

I think of a warrant as the lender's ticket to your upside. The lender takes more risk than a bank would, accepts a lower interest rate than that risk alone would justify, and asks for a small share of the upside if you win. That trade is fine when you price it on purpose and costly when you sign it as boilerplate.

Nothing is issued when you sign. New shares are created only if the lender exercises, and that is when the dilution actually lands.

## The five terms that decide what a warrant really costs

The coverage percentage gets all the attention, but in my experience it is only one of five levers. Here is how I would read the warrant line in any term sheet:

1. **Coverage percentage.** Check whether it applies to the full commitment or only to what you actually draw. If you never draw the whole facility, coverage on the commitment means paying for capital you never used.
2. **Strike price.** The higher the strike, the less the warrant is worth to the lender.
3. **Term.** A longer term gives the lender more time for an exit, so it makes the warrant more valuable.
4. **Exercise method.** Cooley GO explains the cashless option: "Since the value of the exercise price is ‘netted out’ from the shares that the holder receives upon exercise, this is sometimes called a ‘net exercise.’" ([Cooley GO](https://www.cooleygo.com/what-you-should-know-about-warrants/))
5. **Share class and sale treatment.** Know exactly which stock the warrant buys and what happens to it if you sell the company.

## Why the headline percentage is not your dilution

The number on the term sheet is not the share of the company you give up, and I would never let a founder read it that way. Coverage is a percentage of the loan. The lender still has to pay the strike or net it out, so your real cost is the gap between what your shares are worth at exit and the strike price. A higher coverage at a higher strike can cost you less than a lower coverage at a low one.

If you want a sense of what lenders ask for, treat any published range as a starting point. Underridge's venture debt underwriting guide says coverage "often runs in a range of about 5% to 15% of the loan amount" ([Underridge](https://www.underridge.com/underwriting/venture-debt)). That is one publisher's observation, not a rule for your deal.

## Warrants when you have not raised a priced round

This is the part almost every guide skips, and it is the part that matters most for the founders I work with. Most explanations assume the strike is simply set at your last round's price. If you are bootstrapped, or have not raised in years, there is no such price, so the strike has to be negotiated.

ValueAdd VC's glossary notes that lender warrants "aren't subject to the same vesting or 409A pricing rules" as employee options, because "the strike price is negotiated directly as part of the underlying deal." ([ValueAdd VC](https://valueaddvc.com/vc-glossary/warrant)) The strike could be fair market value at grant, a formula, or the price of a future series of preferred stock. That last option deserves care: if the future round never happens, you need to know what the warrant converts into.

For a steadily growing GovTech company, it is also worth asking whether you need a warrant at all. Some growth debt structures carry none; see [how much growth debt a GovTech software company can take on](https://www.lambdavp.com/blog/how-much-growth-debt-govtech-software-company) before you compare offers.

## What to negotiate

I treat every warrant term as negotiable, and the best time to push is when you have more than one term sheet. Start with coverage, then the strike, then the term.

On the share class, I want the warrant to name exactly which stock it buys. If it is vague, or lets the lender pick the class later, your cap table gets murky and pricing your next financing gets harder. I also ask for the warrant to terminate automatically in a sale, so a lender whose loan is already repaid cannot hold up the deal.

Finally, have counsel compute the actual share count the warrant produces under the document's own definitions, not just the stated percentage.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is warrant coverage?

Warrant coverage is the size of the warrant a lender receives alongside a loan, expressed as a percentage of the loan amount rather than of the company. The warrant gives the lender the right, but not the obligation, to buy shares at a fixed strike price before an expiration date.

### Does warrant coverage mean giving up that percentage of the company?

No. Coverage is a share of the loan, not of the company. No shares change hands at signing, the lender still pays the strike price or nets it out on exercise, and exercise only makes sense if the shares are worth more than the strike.

### How is the strike price set?

It is often tied to the price per share of the most recent priced equity round. It can also be fair market value at grant, a formula, or the price of a future series of preferred stock. Each choice changes how much the warrant is worth to the lender.

### How do warrants work for a company that has never raised a priced round?

There is no last round price to anchor to, so the strike is negotiated directly. Lender warrants are generally not subject to the same 409A pricing rules as employee options. A clearly defined fair value strike is usually safer than one tied to a future round that may never happen.

### When do lenders exercise warrants?

Typically around a sale or public offering, if the share price is above the strike. Many warrants are exercised automatically just before a sale if the price is above the strike, or are assumed by the buyer. If no exit happens before expiration, the warrant expires unused.

### What is a cashless exercise?

Cooley GO describes cashless exercise as the holder receiving fewer shares instead of paying the exercise price in cash, which is why it is also called a net exercise. For a private company, cashless exercise is often limited to a sale or public offering.

### What warrant terms can be negotiated?

Coverage, strike price, term, whether coverage applies to committed or drawn amounts, exercise mechanics, and what happens in a sale. The share class matters too: a vague class, or one the lender can choose, can create uncertainty on the cap table.

### Is growth debt available with no warrants at all?

Sometimes. Underridge notes that revenue based financing and asset based lines are alternatives for later stage companies that generate revenue. Whether one fits depends on revenue quality, margins, and the lender, so compare the full cost of each structure, not only the warrant line.
