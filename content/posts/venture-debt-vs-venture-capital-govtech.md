# Venture Debt vs Venture Capital for GovTech: What You Give Up

**A VC round costs you ownership on the day it closes. A loan costs you cash every month after. The question is which one your business can actually afford.**

*By Domenic Giovanzana, Founder and General Partner*

I think most GovTech founders frame this choice too narrowly. Venture capital gives you capital you never repay, in exchange for shares and some control. Debt lets you keep your ownership, but you repay it on schedule no matter how the quarter goes. Venture debt sits in between: a loan that often carries a small equity component for the lender. For a steadily growing GovTech company with sticky government contracts, debt often fits better than VC, provided the cash flow can carry the payments.

*This article is general information, not legal, tax, or investment advice.*

## The three options in one sentence each

I find it helps to strip each option down to what you give and what you get. HubSpot for Startups puts the core tradeoff this way: debt "preserves control but requires regular payments regardless of performance." ([HubSpot for Startups](https://www.hubspot.com/startups/fundraising/equity-vs-debt-financing))

**Venture capital.** You sell ownership shares; the same guide notes there is no obligation to repay if the company fails, but every round dilutes your stake.

**Venture debt.** The guide describes it as similar in structure to a bank loan, often paired with a small equity component for the lender. Warrants are rights to buy shares later, and they sometimes come attached to a loan.

**Loans with bullet payments and revenue based financing.** Loans you repay with interest. With a bullet payment, the principal comes due in one lump sum at maturity ([Touro University open textbook](https://pressbooks.pub/introductiontofinancialanalysis/chapter/11-20-loans-the-conventional-mortgage/)). The startup guide quoted above describes revenue based financing as repaid from a fixed percentage of future revenue, and notes it is still a loan that must be repaid.

## Why venture debt may not be available to a bootstrapped GovTech company

Here is the catch for the founders I talk to most: classic venture debt is built for companies that already have VC backing. The same guide says that after a Series A, you "can unlock debt options," including venture debt, credit lines, and revenue based financing. ([equity vs debt guide](https://www.hubspot.com/startups/fundraising/equity-vs-debt-financing))

That does not close the door on debt. The guide also notes that lenders generally want proven revenue and predictable cash flow. A GovTech company with years of renewals often has exactly that, which makes loans with bullet payments and revenue based financing the more natural comparison.

## What lenders look at when your customers are governments

In my experience, government customers tend to stick once a system is in place, but lenders will not take that on faith. When I review a government contract, I start with the exit doors. Federal contracts can be ended for convenience: the standard clause lets the government terminate "if the Contracting Officer determines that a termination is in the Government's interest" ([FAR 52.249-2](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/52.249-2), [FAR 49.101](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/49.101)). Agencies also cannot commit money that has not been appropriated ([31 U.S.C. 1341](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/1341)), which is why multi year deals often depend on each year's budget.

Next, check whether the receivable can actually back a loan. Federal contract payments generally cannot be transferred, except through the Assignment of Claims Act, which lets a contractor assign payments to a bank or other financing institution when the contract allows it and the conditions are met ([41 U.S.C. 6305](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/41/6305), [FAR 32.802](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/32.802)).

Then there is concentration and timing. If one agency is most of your revenue, a single budget fight or termination can stall the whole business, and long collection cycles make every month of runway more expensive. Walk into any lender conversation with your contract terms, renewal history, funded backlog, and how fast each customer actually pays. If payments run late, know your rights under [prompt payment laws](https://www.lambdavp.com/blog/prompt-payment-laws-govtech-software-vendors).

## When debt fits and when VC fits

I tell founders to decide based on the business they actually want to run, not the one a pitch deck describes. The equity vs debt guide quoted earlier puts it plainly: "Choose debt when you have predictable revenue streams, want to maintain full ownership and control, prefer sustainable growth over hypergrowth, or have strong financials and credit history." ([equity vs debt guide](https://www.hubspot.com/startups/fundraising/equity-vs-debt-financing))

On the other side, the guide says equity fits companies chasing fast growth, industry dominance, or large liquidity events, and companies that are prerevenue or cannot handle regular debt payments.

## Venture capital, venture debt and loans with bullet payments compared

*Typical structures described by the sources cited; terms vary by lender and deal.*

| | Venture capital round | Venture debt | Loan with bullet payments or revenue based financing |
|---|---|---|---|
| What the provider gets | Ownership shares | Interest, repayment, and often a small equity component | Interest and repayment |
| Ownership effect | Dilution at every round | Small and contingent | Ownership kept |
| Repayment | No obligation to repay | Required regardless of performance | Required, though revenue based payments move with revenue |
| Control | Some control exchanged for support | Kept, as long as payments are timely | Kept, as long as payments are timely |
| Typical stage | Often the only option before revenue | After a Series A | Proven revenue and cash flow |
| Best fit | Fast growth, large liquidity events | Runway between equity rounds | Predictable revenue, owners who want control |

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between venture debt and venture capital?

Venture capital buys shares, so your ownership is diluted, but there is no repayment obligation. Venture debt is a loan you repay with interest no matter how the business performs. One startup financing guide describes venture debt as structured like a bank loan, often with a small equity component for the lender.

### Can a bootstrapped GovTech company get venture debt?

Often not in the classic form. One startup financing guide places venture debt after a Series A. A company without venture backing will usually look at loans or revenue based financing instead.

### What is revenue based financing?

One startup financing guide describes revenue based financing as a flexible loan repaid using a fixed percentage of future revenue, so payments scale with performance. It notes that it is still a loan and must be repaid. Lenders generally want proven revenue and cash flow.

### Do government contracts help or hurt when borrowing?

They can do both. In my experience government customers tend to renew, but lenders will look at termination for convenience rights, non appropriation risk, customer concentration, and how long collections take. Federal receivables can be assigned to a lender only under the Assignment of Claims Act. Bring your renewal history and contract terms to any lender conversation.

### When is VC the better choice than debt?

One startup financing guide says equity fits companies chasing fast growth, industry dominance, or large liquidity events. Debt fits predictable revenue, owners who want control, and sustainable growth. A steadily growing company with long term government customers often matches the debt profile, if cash flow can carry the payments.
