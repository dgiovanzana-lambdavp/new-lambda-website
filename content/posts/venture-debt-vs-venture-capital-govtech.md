# Venture Debt vs Venture Capital for GovTech: Term Loans, Warrants, and What You Give Up

**A VC round costs you ownership on the day it closes. A loan costs you cash every month after. The question is which one your business can actually afford.**

*By Domenic Giovanzana, Founder and General Partner, Lambda Capital. Last updated October 14, 2026.*

I think most GovTech founders frame this choice too narrowly. Venture capital gives you capital you never repay, in exchange for shares and some control. Debt lets you keep your ownership, but you repay it on schedule no matter how the quarter goes. Venture debt sits in between: a loan that usually comes with warrants, sized as a percentage of the loan. For a steadily growing GovTech company with sticky government contracts, debt often fits better than VC, provided the cash flow can carry the payments.

*This article is general information, not legal, tax, or investment advice.*

## The three options in one sentence each

I find it helps to strip each option down to what you give and what you get. HubSpot for Startups puts the core tradeoff this way: debt "preserves control but requires regular payments regardless of performance." ([HubSpot for Startups](https://www.hubspot.com/startups/fundraising/equity-vs-debt-financing))

**Venture capital.** You sell ownership shares; HubSpot for Startups notes there is no obligation to repay if the company fails, but every round dilutes your stake.

**Venture debt.** HubSpot for Startups describes it as similar in structure to a bank loan, usually paired with warrants or other small equity kickers for the lender.

**Term loans and revenue based financing.** Loans you repay with interest. HubSpot for Startups describes revenue based financing as repaid from a fixed percentage of future revenue, and notes it is still a loan that must be repaid.

## How warrant coverage works: the minimal equity middle ground

The warrant percentage is the number that scares founders, and in my experience it is usually misread. Venturemage is clear on this: "Warrant coverage is expressed as a percentage of the loan amount, not the company valuation." ([Venturemage](https://venturemage.com/warrant-coverage-venture-debt))

In the same guide's example, 10% coverage on a $2 million loan gives the lender warrants worth $200,000 at the agreed strike price. A warrant is a right, not an obligation, to buy shares at that fixed price before an expiration date. If it is never exercised, the guide notes, no dilution occurs.

**How much dilution?** Venturemage says the average venture debt deal results in roughly 1 to 2 percent total equity dilution if the lender exercises, while its own FAQ puts the range at 1 to 3 percent; treat both as the publisher's estimate, not independent data.

The real cost depends on what your company is worth later. Startup Fortune explains that the strike price is locked when the loan closes and never resets upward ([Startup Fortune](https://startupfortune.com/how-does-venture-debt-warrant-coverage-work-and-why-it-costs-more/)), so the better you do, the more the warrant is worth to the lender.

## Why venture debt may not be available to a bootstrapped GovTech company

Here is the catch for the founders I talk to most: classic venture debt is built for companies that already have VC backing. HubSpot for Startups says that after a Series A, you "can unlock debt options," including venture debt, credit lines, and revenue based financing. ([HubSpot for Startups](https://www.hubspot.com/startups/fundraising/equity-vs-debt-financing))

The same warrant guide adds that the best venture debt terms come within 6 to 12 months of closing an equity round. If you have never raised one, that window does not exist for you.

That does not close the door on debt. HubSpot for Startups notes that lenders generally want proven revenue and predictable cash flow. A GovTech company with years of renewals often has exactly that, which makes term loans and revenue based financing the more natural comparison.

## What lenders look at when your customers are governments

In my experience, government customers tend to stick once a system is in place, but lenders will not take that on faith. When I review a government contract, I start with the exit doors. Federal contracts can be ended for convenience: the standard clause lets the government terminate "if the Contracting Officer determines that a termination is in the Government's interest" ([FAR 52.249-2](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/52.249-2), [FAR 49.101](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/49.101)). Agencies also cannot commit money that has not been appropriated ([31 U.S.C. 1341](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/1341)), which is why multi year deals often depend on each year's budget.

Next, check whether the receivable can actually back a loan. Federal contract payments generally cannot be transferred, except through the Assignment of Claims Act, which lets a contractor assign payments to a bank or other financing institution when the contract allows it and the conditions are met ([41 U.S.C. 6305](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/41/6305), [FAR 32.802](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/32.802)).

Then there is concentration and timing. If one agency is most of your revenue, a single budget fight or termination can stall the whole business, and long collection cycles make every month of runway more expensive. Walk into any lender conversation with your contract terms, renewal history, funded backlog, and how fast each customer actually pays. If payments run late, know your rights under [prompt payment laws](https://www.lambdavp.com/blog/prompt-payment-laws-govtech-software-vendors).

## When debt fits and when VC fits

I tell founders to decide based on the business they actually want to run, not the one a pitch deck describes. HubSpot for Startups puts it plainly: "Choose debt when you have predictable revenue streams, want to maintain full ownership and control, prefer sustainable growth over hypergrowth, or have strong financials and credit history." ([HubSpot for Startups](https://www.hubspot.com/startups/fundraising/equity-vs-debt-financing))

On the other side, HubSpot for Startups says equity fits companies chasing fast growth, industry dominance, or large liquidity events, and companies that are prerevenue or cannot handle regular debt payments.

|---|---|---|---|
| What the provider gets | Ownership shares | Interest, repayment, and usually warrants | Interest and repayment |

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between venture debt and venture capital?

Venture capital buys shares, so your ownership is diluted, but there is no repayment obligation. Venture debt is a loan you repay with interest no matter how the business performs, and lenders usually add warrants. HubSpot for Startups describes venture debt as structured like a bank loan, with warrants or other small equity kickers.

### What is warrant coverage?

Warrant coverage is the size of the warrant the lender receives, expressed as a percentage of the loan amount, not of the company. In Venturemage's example, 10% coverage on a $2 million loan gives the lender warrants worth $200,000 at the agreed strike price.

### How much dilution do venture debt warrants usually cause?

Venturemage says the average venture debt deal results in roughly 1 to 2 percent total equity dilution if the lender exercises, and its own FAQ gives 1 to 3 percent. Treat that as the publisher's estimate, not independent data. Your real cost depends on your valuation and whether the warrants are ever exercised.

### Can a bootstrapped GovTech company get venture debt?

Often not in the classic form. HubSpot for Startups places venture debt after a Series A, and one warrant guide says the best terms come within 6 to 12 months of an equity round. A company without venture backing will usually look at loans or revenue based financing instead.

### What is revenue based financing?

HubSpot for Startups describes revenue based financing as a flexible loan repaid using a fixed percentage of future revenue, so payments scale with performance. It notes that it is still a loan and must be repaid. Lenders generally want proven revenue and cash flow.

### Do government contracts help or hurt when borrowing?

They can do both. In my experience government customers tend to renew, but lenders will look at termination for convenience rights, non appropriation risk, customer concentration, and how long collections take. Federal receivables can be assigned to a lender only under the Assignment of Claims Act. Bring your renewal history and contract terms to any lender conversation.

### When is VC the better choice than debt?

HubSpot for Startups says equity fits companies chasing fast growth, industry dominance, or large liquidity events. Debt fits predictable revenue, owners who want control, and sustainable growth. A steadily growing company with long term government customers often matches the debt profile, if cash flow can carry the payments.
