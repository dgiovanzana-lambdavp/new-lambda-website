# The Assignment of Claims Act Explained

**A signed government contract is your most valuable asset. Whether you can borrow against it depends on a law most software founders have never read.**

*By Domenic Giovanzana, Founder and General Partner*

The Assignment of Claims Act lets a federal contractor assign the money due under a contract to a bank or other financing institution, typically as security for a loan. The contract itself is not assigned, only the right to be paid. Under FAR 32.802 the contract must total $1,000 or more and must not prohibit assignment, and the assignee must send written notice to the contracting officer, any surety, and the disbursing officer. After that, payments go to the assignee until a release is filed. State and local contracts follow their own law and contract terms.

*This article is general information, not legal, tax, or investment advice. Have counsel review any assignment.*

## What the Assignment of Claims Act is

When founders first hear the phrase, I usually explain it as permission. By default, a contractor cannot simply pledge what the federal government owes it the way it might pledge a commercial receivable. The Act creates a defined path to do that.

The rules sit in the Federal Acquisition Regulation, which opens its subpart on the topic this way: "This subpart prescribes policies and procedures for the assignment of claims under the Assignment of Claims Act of 1940, as amended, (31 U.S.C. 3727, 41 U.S.C. 6305)" ([FAR subpart 32.8](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/subpart-32.8)) In plain terms, the Act is the law, and FAR subpart 32.8 is how agencies apply it.

## The conditions an assignment has to meet

What surprises most CEOs I talk to is how specific the conditions are. FAR 32.802 says "a contractor may assign moneys due or to become due under a contract if all the following conditions are met," and then lists them ([FAR 32.802](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/subpart-32.8)):

1. The contract specifies payments aggregating $1,000 or more.
2. The assignment is made to a bank, trust company, or other financing institution, including any federal lending agency.
3. The contract does not prohibit the assignment.
4. Unless the contract expressly permits otherwise, the assignment covers all unpaid amounts under the contract, is made to only one party (or to one party acting as agent or trustee for several), and is not subject to further assignment.
5. The assignee sends a written notice of assignment, with a true copy of the assignment instrument, to the contracting officer or agency head, the surety on any bond, and the disbursing officer.

The fourth condition matters more than it looks. By default, you cannot assign a slice of one contract's payments; it is all unpaid amounts or nothing, unless the contract says otherwise.

## How the process works, step by step

My own way of picturing the process is as paperwork that changes the payee on file. Here is the sequence the regulation describes:

1. **The financing institution reviews the contract.** Whether the contract prohibits assignment is the first question.
2. **The contractor signs an assignment instrument.** It transfers the right to be paid under that contract.
3. **The assignee sends notice.** The written notice and a true copy of the instrument go to the contracting officer or agency head, any surety, and the disbursing officer.
4. **The government acknowledges it.** From then on, payments under the contract go to the assignee.
5. **A release ends it.** When the financing institution releases the contractor, the contractor files a written notice of release and a copy of the release instrument with the same offices, and payments return to the contractor.

## What changes for your company after an assignment

From the inside, I have found that very little changes day to day. Your team still delivers the software, supports users, and invoices on the same schedule. The difference is where the money lands.

One term is worth knowing by name: the no setoff commitment. The regulation defines it as a contract term under which payments to the assignee will not be reduced to liquidate the contractor's debts to the government. An agency may include it only after a determination of need, so many contracts will not carry one. When a contract has no such commitment, the government keeps its usual right to apply setoffs. For how late payment interest works on federal invoices, see our post on [prompt payment laws for GovTech vendors](https://www.lambdavp.com/blog/prompt-payment-laws-govtech-software-vendors).

## State and local contracts work differently

This is the section I would want every state and local vendor to read twice. The Assignment of Claims Act governs federal contracts only. State and local contracts follow state law and the assignment clause in the contract itself.

Before assuming a state or local receivable can be pledged, read the assignment clause and have counsel review it.

## How assignment fits with the payment wait

Long payment cycles are the reason this topic comes up at all, and I see them in nearly every GovTech company I talk to. For many federal invoices, the due date is the later of the 30th day after a proper invoice is received or the 30th day after acceptance, under [FAR 32.904](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/subpart-32.9). State and local timelines vary.

## Federal vs state and local assignment of contract payments

*Based on the sources cited; rules vary by jurisdiction and contract.*

| | Federal contracts | State and local contracts |
|---|---|---|
| Governing rule | Assignment of Claims Act and FAR subpart 32.8 | State law and the contract's assignment clause |
| Who can be assignee | Bank, trust company, or other financing institution | Depends on jurisdiction and contract |
| Contract must allow it | Yes; the contract must not prohibit assignment | Depends on the contract's assignment clause |
| Notice | Written notice to contracting officer, surety, and disbursing officer | Agency consent where the contract requires it |
| What is assigned | The right to be paid, not the contract | Varies by contract |
| How it ends | Notice of release filed with the same offices | Varies by contract |

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the Assignment of Claims Act?

It is the federal law that lets a contractor assign money due or to become due under a federal contract to a bank, trust company, or other financing institution. It dates to 1940, is codified at 31 U.S.C. 3727 and 41 U.S.C. 6305, and is implemented in FAR subpart 32.8.

### Is the contract itself assigned?

No. What moves is the right to receive payment. The contractor still performs the work, and the assignment changes where the government sends the money.

### What conditions must an assignment meet?

The contract must specify payments aggregating $1,000 or more and must not prohibit assignment. The assignee must be a bank, trust company, or other financing institution. Unless the contract expressly permits otherwise, the assignment covers all unpaid amounts, goes to one party (or one agent for several), and cannot be assigned again.

### Who receives notice of an assignment?

The assignee sends a written notice of assignment, with a true copy of the assignment instrument, to the contracting officer or agency head, any surety on a bond for the contract, and the disbursing officer designated in the contract.

### How does an assignment end?

When the financing institution releases the contractor, the contractor files a written notice of release and a copy of the release instrument with the same offices that received the original notice. Payments then return to the contractor.

### What is a no setoff commitment?

It is a contract term under which payments to the assignee will not be reduced to collect debts the contractor owes the government. An agency may include one only after a determination of need, so many contracts do not have one.

### Does the Assignment of Claims Act apply to state and local contracts?

No. It covers federal contracts. State and local contracts follow state law and the contract's own assignment clause.

### How long do federal agencies have to pay an invoice?

Under FAR 32.904, the due date for many invoice payments is the later of the 30th day after the designated billing office receives a proper invoice or the 30th day after government acceptance. An assignment does not change that clock; it changes who is paid.
