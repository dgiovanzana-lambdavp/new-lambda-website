# Government Contract Financing for Software Companies: How to Fund the Gap Between Award and Payment

*By Domenic Giovanzana, Founding Partner, Lambda Capital. Last updated October 10, 2026.*

Software companies that sell to government can bridge the gap between a signed contract and the first payment with four main tools: early payment programs on approved invoices, invoice factoring, working capital or recurring revenue credit lines, and contract-backed capital that funds delivery before you can invoice. Which one fits depends on three questions.

Has the agency approved your invoice yet? Is the revenue recurring subscription or one-time implementation? Does your contract allow payments to be assigned to a lender?

I spend a lot of my time with GovTech founders who have done the hard part. They won the RFP, signed the contract, and started delivery. Then they learn the agency's payment clock is not their payroll clock. This post is the guide I wish more of them had before they signed. At Lambda Capital we provide capital to vertical software companies, and GovTech is one of our focus areas, so I will tell you where we fit. Most of this post is about choosing the right tool, whoever provides it.

## Why government customers pay late even with a signed contract

Government customers pay slowly because of how public money moves, not because they do not intend to pay.

Lunch, which runs early payment programs for cities and school districts, puts it well: "Government payment timelines are structural, not personal. Budget cycles, multi-step approvals, and manual processes slow payments even when agencies intend to pay promptly." ([Lunch](https://www.lunchpayments.com/resources/net-30-vs-net-60-vs-net-90-payment-terms-explained))

A few things stack up:

- **Stated terms are a best case.** Lunch writes that "most municipalities operate on net-30 to net-60 terms, but actual payment can take 60-90+ days once internal approvals and processing are factored in." ([Lunch](https://www.lunchpayments.com/resources/cash-flow-management-government-contractors))
- **Approval chains.** One invoice can need sign-off from the department that bought your software, the budget office, and whoever cuts the check.
- **Batch payment runs.** In Lunch's words, "an approved invoice might sit for days before a check is cut." ([Lunch](https://www.lunchpayments.com/resources/net-30-vs-net-60-vs-net-90-payment-terms-explained))
- **Fiscal year boundaries.** An invoice that lands at year end can wait for new appropriations.

For software vendors there is one more delay that gets ignored: the time between signature and your first invoice. A typical sequence is award, kickoff, implementation, go-live or acceptance milestone, invoice, approval, payment. You are paying engineers and cloud bills through all of it.

How long is that whole stretch? Be careful with the numbers you see online. FundBizPro, an educational site, states that on federal work "the actual gap from award date to first dollar received is often 90-180 days." ([FundBizPro](https://fundbizpro.com/government-contracts/guides/sba-payment-government-contract-cash-flow)) Verter, a blog focused on business-to-government startups, writes that "payment delays of 90 to 180 days are standard operating procedure." ([Verter](https://verter.studio/blog/venture-capital-accelerators-and-b2g-startups---the-infrastructure-gap-in-financial-planning-for-business-to-government-companies)) Those are vendor and blog claims, not audited data, and the first one is about federal contracts, not state and local. I have not found a reliable published dataset on actual SLED days-to-pay for software vendors. The only number that matters for your plan is your own agency's real payment history.

## How big is your cash gap? A worked example

Your peak cash need is roughly your monthly delivery cost multiplied by the number of months until the first payment actually lands, minus any upfront payment the contract gives you.

Here is an illustrative example. The company and figures are made up to show the math, not drawn from any real vendor.

A GovTech SaaS company signs a county contract. Delivery (implementation team, support, hosting) costs **$80,000 per month**. The contract bills at go-live, which is planned for **month 4**. Stated terms are net 60.

| Scenario | First payment arrives | Months of cost carried | Peak cash need |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paid on stated terms (60 days after invoice) | Month 6 | 6 | $480,000 |
| Paid 90 days after invoice | Month 7 | 7 | $560,000 |
| Paid 180 days after invoice | Month 10 | 10 | $800,000 |

Two lessons from the math:

1. **The implementation period is often bigger than the payment delay.** Four months of delivery before you can even invoice is a large part of the gap, and factoring or early payment cannot touch it.
2. **Growth makes it worse.** Every new contract you sign starts its own gap. Lunch makes the same point: "winning more work can actually make your cash position worse, not better." ([Lunch](https://www.lunchpayments.com/resources/net-30-vs-net-60-vs-net-90-payment-terms-explained))

Build this as a weekly cash forecast by contract, use the payment history you actually have with each agency, and find the lowest balance. That number is how much financing you need, and when.

## Your financing options, compared

The right tool depends mostly on whether you already have an approved invoice.

| Option | What it funds | Needs an approved invoice? | Debt on your balance sheet? | Cost signal (source, as of Oct 2026) | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Municipal early payment program | Approved invoices from agencies that participate | Yes | No, per providers it is a sale of the receivable | Flat fee per invoice; Lunch cites "typically 1-3%" ([Lunch](https://www.lunchpayments.com/resources/what-is-municipal-early-payment-program)) | Vendors whose agency already offers a program |
| Invoice factoring | Submitted or accepted invoices | Yes, generally | Usually structured as a sale; terms vary | "1% to 4% of invoice value for each 30 days the invoice stays outstanding" ([Flexent](https://flexent.com/government-contract-factoring/)) | Large one-time invoices: implementation, licenses, services |
| Bank or SBA line of credit | General working capital | No | Yes | Lower cost, but CyberX Gov notes it is "slow to approve; backward-looking underwriting" ([CyberX Gov](https://cyberxgovsolutions.com/government-contract-financing-cash-flow/)) | Mature companies that set it up early |
| ARR or MRR line | Working capital against recurring revenue | No | Yes | LaunchAdvisor calls it "often the cheapest option if you qualify" for pure SaaS ([LaunchAdvisor](https://www.launchadvisor.co/guides/invoice-factoring-vs-accounts-receivable-financing-software-publishers-saas)) | SaaS with steady subscription revenue |
| Contract-backed capital | Delivery costs before you can invoice | No, it underwrites the signed contract | Usually yes, a loan | Varies; CyberX Gov notes it "requires awarded contracts to underwrite" ([CyberX Gov](https://cyberxgovsolutions.com/government-contract-financing-cash-flow/)) | Implementation-heavy contracts and multi-contract backlogs |
| Grants and SBIR/STTR | Research and development | No | No | Non-dilutive, but tied to R&D scope and timelines | Product R&D, not working capital on a signed contract |

Cost figures in this table are marketing numbers from the providers themselves, as of Oct 2026, and can change. A few notes from the founder's side of the table.

**Early payment programs** are hard to beat when they are available, but they only work on invoices your agency has already approved, and only if your agency participates. Ask your agency's finance office whether one exists. It costs nothing to ask.

**Factoring** works best on a big implementation or license invoice. It is awkward for subscription revenue. LaunchAdvisor's view is that factoring "has limited use for core subscription revenue but can be considered for large, non-recurring professional services or license fee invoices." ([LaunchAdvisor](https://www.launchadvisor.co/guides/invoice-factoring-vs-accounts-receivable-financing-software-publishers-saas)) Factors also usually need the agency to send payment to them, which brings up assignment (more below).

**Bank lines** are cheap and slow. The time to open one is before you need it.

**ARR lines** fit companies with a stable base of recurring revenue. If most of your near-term revenue is one large contract still in implementation, you may not have the recurring base these lenders size against yet.

**Contract-backed capital** is the only option on this list built for the stretch before the first invoice. A lender underwriting a signed government contract typically looks at funded scope, your ability to deliver, gross margin, and where the repayment comes from. CyberX Gov describes this approach as one that "underwrites your contract backlog, the awarded but not-yet-billed dollars, plus your past performance." ([CyberX Gov](https://cyberxgovsolutions.com/government-contract-financing-cash-flow/))

## Can you assign a government contract's payments to a lender?

For federal contracts, yes, if you meet the Assignment of Claims Act conditions. For state and local contracts, it depends on the jurisdiction and your contract.

**Federal.** FAR 32.802 says "a contractor may assign moneys due or to become due under a contract" if all of its conditions are met. These include: the contract specifies payments of $1,000 or more, the assignment goes to "a bank, trust company, or other financing institution," the contract does not prohibit assignment, and the assignee sends written notice to the contracting officer, any surety, and the disbursing officer. ([FAR 32.802](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/32.802))

**State and local.** There is no single rule. Some factoring firms say SLED assignment rules are "generally simpler" ([Flexent](https://flexent.com/government-contract-factoring/)), but do not take that as a given. Read your contract's assignment clause and talk to counsel before you sign anything that changes where an agency sends payment.

**A practical point.** Assignment also means asking the agency's finance team to change where it sends payment, which adds a step on their side. If you would rather leave the agency's process untouched, look for financing where the agency keeps paying you directly and you repay the lender from your own account.

**Subscription vs one-time invoices.** Annual subscription invoices, implementation fees, and license fees behave differently. One-time invoices are easier to factor. Recurring and contracted revenue is better suited to a credit line or contract-backed loan.

## Factoring vs early payment vs contract-backed capital: which should a GovTech vendor choose?

Pick based on where you are in the contract's life, not on headline price alone.

- **You have an approved invoice and your agency runs an early payment program.** Start there.
- **You have a large approved or submitted one-time invoice, no program, and your contract allows assignment.** Factoring can work. Compare the total fee at the agency's real payment speed, not the stated term.
- **You are mid-implementation and cannot invoice yet.** Early payment and factoring do not help. You need a line of credit you already have, an ARR line if you have the recurring base, or contract-backed capital.
- **You have several signed contracts and renewals coming and want one facility.** A term loan sized to the contracted backlog is usually simpler than factoring invoice by invoice.
- **You do not want to involve the agency at all.** Favor structures where nothing about the agency's payment process changes.

Whatever you choose, compare total cost in dollars over the period you will actually use the money, and read the term sheet for personal guarantees, minimums, warrants, and what happens if the agency pays later than expected.

## How to negotiate and structure contracts to shrink the gap

The cheapest financing is the gap you never create.

- **Bill by milestone or monthly, not at the end.** Lunch notes that payment terms are often fixed by procurement, but "you can negotiate the structure of payments," such as progress billing and more frequent invoicing. ([Lunch](https://www.lunchpayments.com/resources/cash-flow-management-government-contractors))
- **Ask about early payment programs before you sign.**
- **Price the cost of waiting into your bid.** If you know the agency pays in 120 days, the cost of carrying that should be in your price.
- **Know your state's prompt payment rules.** Many states require interest on late payments, with their own deadlines and claim steps. Two examples: New York State Finance Law §179-f (state agencies) requires the State to pay most vendors "within 30 days" and 15 days for qualified small businesses, with interest possible after the due date ([NY Office of the State Comptroller](https://www.osc.ny.gov/state-agencies/gfo/chapter-xii/xii5i-prompt-payment-interest)). Minnesota Statute 471.425 sets a standard payment period of 35 days for municipalities whose boards meet at least monthly, and 45 days for those that do not ([Minnesota Revisor of Statutes](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/2025/cite/471.425)). Rules differ by state and by agency type, so check yours.
- **Set up financing before you win.** The worst time to look for capital is after you have signed and payroll is due.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a software company get financing against a signed government contract?

Yes. Pre-invoice options include contract-backed loans, purchase order or work order financing, and existing working capital lines. Invoice factoring and early payment programs generally need a submitted or approved invoice. Lenders underwriting a signed contract typically look at funded scope, your ability to deliver, gross margin, and the source of repayment.

### How long do state and local governments actually take to pay software vendors?

It varies by agency. Lunch reports most municipalities set net 30 to net 60 terms, but actual payment "can take 60-90+ days once internal approvals and processing are factored in." Add implementation time before your first invoice. Plan with each agency's real payment history, not the contract's stated terms.

### What is the difference between early payment programs and invoice factoring?

Early payment programs buy invoices an agency has already approved, through a program the agency participates in, usually for a flat fee per invoice. Factoring is a facility the vendor sets up with a factor, often with fees that grow with time outstanding. Flexent quotes 1% to 4% of invoice value per 30 days (as of Oct 2026, a provider marketing figure).

### Can SaaS companies use invoice factoring on government contracts?

Yes, but it fits best on large one-time invoices such as implementation, integration, or license fees. LaunchAdvisor says factoring has limited use for core subscription revenue. Recurring revenue is usually better served by an ARR-based line of credit or a loan sized against contracted revenue. Check your contract's assignment clause first.

### Is government contract financing debt?

It depends on the structure. Early payment programs and factoring are usually described by providers as purchases of your receivables, not loans. Lines of credit, ARR lines, and contract-backed term loans are debt. Read the term sheet for recourse, personal guarantees, minimums, and warrants before you decide which you prefer.

### Can I assign my government contract payments to a lender?

On federal contracts, yes, if the Assignment of Claims Act conditions in FAR 32.802 are met, including assignment to a bank or other financing institution and written notice to the contracting officer, surety, and disbursing officer. On state and local contracts, it depends on the jurisdiction and your contract's assignment clause, so ask counsel.

### What happens if the government pays late?

Many states have prompt payment laws that require interest on late payments. New York, for example, requires state agencies to pay most vendors within 30 days, with interest possible after the due date. Minnesota sets 35 or 45 day standards for municipalities. Each law has its own deadlines and claim steps, so check yours.

### How do I avoid running out of cash on a Net 90 or Net 180 contract?

Build a weekly cash forecast using each agency's real payment history and find your lowest projected balance. Negotiate milestone or monthly billing, ask whether the agency runs an early payment program, price the cost of waiting into your bid, and put a financing facility in place before you sign, not after payroll is at risk.
